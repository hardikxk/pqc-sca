\begin{abstract}
The imminent threat of quantum cryptanalysis has driven the global standardization of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC), with lattice-based primitives such as ML-KEM (\href{https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/203/final}{NIST FIPS 203}) replacing legacy public-key algorithms. While mathematically robust against quantum polynomial-time algorithms, physical implementations of ML-KEM deployed on embedded microcontrollers remain vulnerable to Side-Channel Analysis (SCA). Deep Learning-based SCA (DL-SCA) has demonstrated high effectiveness at circumventing algorithmic countermeasures such as Boolean masking by directly modeling multi-share leakage distributions; however, existing evaluation frameworks encounter severe computational and memory bottlenecks when profiling PQC targets. The Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) produces prolonged execution traces ($>40,000$ sample points per trace) that cause out-of-memory crashes ($>16.7\text{ GB}$ for $100,000$ traces) in standard monolithic Python environments. Furthermore, standard dense classification heads suffer from parameter explosion when mapping high-dimensional feature representations. In this paper, we present \textbf{MojoPQC-SCA}, an end-to-end, memory-bounded neural profiling pipeline tailored for post-quantum side-channel evaluation on commodity hardware. Our framework features a 6-stage streaming ingestion engine that couples bounded-memory chunking, zero-phase digital FIR filtering, template cross-correlation alignment, and Point-of-Interest (POI) decimation to 5,000 samples, restricting peak memory footprint to $\le 111.8\text{ MB}$ ($>99.3\%$ reduction). At the model layer, we replace traditional dense classifiers with a quantum-inspired Matrix Product State (MPS) tensor network layer with bond dimension $\chi = 8$, reducing trainable parameters from $14,400$ to $9,226$ ($35.93\%$ parameter reduction, consuming only $4.6\%$ of the $200,000$-parameter budget) while accelerating training by $1.69\times$. Evaluated on both physical ARM Cortex-M4 power traces and controlled NTT leakage benchmarks, our hybrid architecture drives Guessing Entropy monotonically to Rank $1.0$, successfully recovering masked secret polynomial coefficients. Finally, by compiling models to ONNX and applying dynamic symmetric Int8 quantization, we achieve CPU inference latencies of $0.14\text{ ms per trace}$ ($7,142\text{ traces/s}$), providing an auditable and reproducible platform with end-to-end cryptographic SHA-256 verification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The Post-Quantum Transition and Physical Vulnerabilities}
The ongoing development of fault-tolerant quantum computers threatens the mathematical foundations of modern public-key cryptography, including RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) \cite{alagic2020status,alagic2022status}. In response, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) concluded its multi-year Post-Quantum Cryptography standardization process by issuing Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS), establishing ML-KEM (\href{https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/203/final}{FIPS 203}, based on CRYSTALS-Kyber) as the primary standard for general encryption and key encapsulation \cite{nist_fips203,bos2018crystals}, alongside ML-DSA (\href{https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/204/final}{FIPS 204}) for digital signatures \cite{ml_dsa_fips204,ducas2018crystals}, and SLH-DSA (\href{https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/205/final}{FIPS 205}) for stateless hash-based signatures \cite{slh_dsa_fips205}. 

These post-quantum schemes rely on the hardness of high-dimensional lattice problems over module rings, specifically the Module Learning With Errors (M-LWE) and Ring Learning With Errors (R-LWE) problems \cite{regev2005lattices,lyubashevsky2013ideal,peikert2016decade}. While mathematically secure against both classical and quantum adversaries in idealized environments, physical implementations of ML-KEM on microcontrollers, smart cards, and IoT System-on-Chip (SoC) architectures unintentionally radiate sensitive information. Physical side channels, primarily instantaneous power consumption and electromagnetic (EM) emissions, reflect the internal processing of secret keys \cite{kocher1999differential,brier2004correlation,mangard2007power,schindler2005stochastic}. Standardized vulnerability assessment methodologies such as Test Vector Leakage Assessment (TVLA) \cite{goodwill2011tvla} have confirmed pervasive information leakage across physical targets. As demonstrated in recent hardware evaluations on embedded platforms such as the ARM Cortex-M4 \cite{kannwischer2019pqm4}, unmasked and even masked implementations of Kyber can be broken via side-channel analysis if algorithmic defenses are not rigorously applied across all computational stages \cite{amiet2020leakage,ravi2020side,pessl2020masked,ngo2021masked,hamburg2021chosen,fritzmann2020masked}.

\subsection{Deep Learning-Based Side-Channel Analysis (DL-SCA)}
To prevent side-channel leakage, cryptographic engineers deploy algorithmic countermeasures, most notably \textit{Boolean masking} \cite{chari1999towards,barthe2015formal}. Under a $d$-share masking scheme, intermediate sensitive variables are split into $d$ statistically independent randomized shares, ensuring that any statistical moment of order lower than $d$ carries zero correlation with the underlying secret \cite{bronchain2020side}. Traditional side-channel attacks, such as Correlation Power Analysis (CPA) and Template Attacks (TA), require exponential trace counts as $d$ increases and demand precise time-synchronization and manual Point-of-Interest (POI) selection \cite{brier2004correlation,standaert2009unified}.

Deep Learning-based Side-Channel Analysis (DL-SCA) has emerged as a powerful paradigm capable of bypassing these defenses \cite{maghrebi2016deep,cagli2017convolutional,benadjila2020deep}. Following foundational benchmark releases such as the French National Cybersecurity Agency's ASCAD database (\href{https://github.com/ANSSI-FR/ascad}{ASCAD Database}) \cite{ascaddatabase2018,benadjila2020deep}, researchers have demonstrated that neural networks can automatically extract non-linear cross-share interactions from raw or minimally preprocessed waveforms. By formulating key recovery as a supervised classification problem, 1-Dimensional Convolutional Neural Networks (1D-CNNs) provide temporal translation invariance, enabling them to naturally circumvent hardware countermeasures such as clock jitter, random dummy cycle insertions, and phase drift without requiring tedious trace alignment \cite{cagli2017convolutional,kim2019make,perin2020neural,wouters2020revisiting,zaid2020methodology,masure2020comprehensive,robyns2019improving,wu2022removing,picek2023deep}.

\subsection{The Computational Bottleneck of PQC Profiling}
Despite its success on legacy block ciphers like AES-128, applying DL-SCA to post-quantum algorithms introduces severe computational and memory bottlenecks for security evaluators:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Trace Dimensionality:} In AES, an encryption trace covering the sensitive S-Box operation typically spans $1,000$ to $3,000$ sample points. Conversely, ML-KEM operations rely on the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) to perform polynomial multiplications in $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^{256} + 1)$ with modulus $q = 3329$ \cite{bos2018crystals,roychoudhury2019accelerating}. Executing the 7-stage NTT butterfly network on an embedded microcontroller generates traces spanning $20,000$ to over $45,000$ samples per decapsulation \cite{amiet2020leakage,pessl2020masked,ueno2022curse}.
    \item \textbf{Memory Bloat and OOM Failures:} High-level Python frameworks (e.g., NumPy, SciPy, and monolithic PyTorch loaders \cite{paszke2019pytorch}) customarily load entire trace datasets into contiguous memory. A modest profiling campaign of $100,000$ traces of length $L = 41,800$ requires over $16.7\text{ GB}$ of RAM in 32-bit floating-point precision, precipitating Out-Of-Memory (OOM) crashes on standard workstations.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Explosion in Neural Heads:} Mapping high-dimensional convolutional feature representations to class logits using traditional fully-connected (dense) layers introduces hundreds of thousands of trainable parameters. This not only violates low-complexity hardware evaluation constraints (e.g., a $200,000$-parameter budget for edge evaluators) but also leads to severe overfitting in low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) regimes \cite{novikov2015tensorizing}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Proposed Solution: MojoPQC-SCA}
To eliminate these systemic bottlenecks, we propose \textbf{MojoPQC-SCA}, an integrated neural profiling framework engineered for post-quantum side-channel evaluation. Our approach operates simultaneously across two complementary tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Systems Level (Streaming Pipeline):} We formulate a 6-stage memory-bounded streaming pipeline utilizing chunked HDF5 iterators (\href{https://www.hdfgroup.org/HDF5/}{HDF5 Group}) \cite{hdfgroup2020hdf5}, zero-phase digital FIR smoothing, template cross-correlation alignment, and POI decimation to $5,000$ samples. Designed to interoperate with compiled SIMD environments like Mojo (\href{https://www.modular.com/mojo}{Modular Mojo}) \cite{lattner2023mojo}, this pipeline bounds peak RSS consumption to $\le 111.8\text{ MB}$, enabling large-scale trace ingestion on commodity consumer CPUs.
    \item \textbf{Model Level (Quantum-Inspired Tensor Networks):} We replace the dense classification head with a Matrix Product State (MPS) tensor network layer with bond dimension $\chi = 8$. By factorizing the high-order weight tensor into a low-rank core chain \cite{novikov2015tensorizing,stoudenmire2016supervised,cichocki2016tensor,glass2020quantum,oseledets2011tensor,huggins2019towards}, we compress classification parameters from $8,416$ down to $3,242$, yielding an overall model footprint of only $9,226$ parameters ($35.93\%$ parameter reduction over the 1D-CNN baseline).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Summary of Contributions}
The key contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Memory-Bounded Ingestion Architecture:} We develop a 6-stage streaming preprocessing pipeline that guarantees bounded-memory chunking ($<120\text{ MB}$ peak RSS) across datasets of $100,000+$ traces, achieving up to $761.2\text{ traces/s}$ throughput on local CPUs.
    \item \textbf{Quantum-Inspired MPS Neural Profiler:} We design and implement a compact hybrid 1D-CNN + MPS profiling architecture that utilizes only $9,226$ parameters ($4.6\%$ of the $200,000$-parameter budget), preventing overfitting while speeding up training epochs by $1.69\times$.
    \item \textbf{Key Recovery on Masked ML-KEM-512:} We demonstrate empirical Guessing Entropy ($GE$) convergence from random guess ($GE \approx 128$) to Rank $1.0$ across physical ARM Cortex-M4 acquisition traces from the open Zenodo repository \cite{zenodo_kyber_dataset,danvers2022side}, verifying that the MPS layer effectively captures multi-share correlations in masked lattice primitives.
    \item \textbf{Quantized Edge Deployment and Auditability:} We deploy the trained models via ONNX Runtime (\href{https://onnxruntime.ai/}{ONNX Runtime}) \cite{onnxruntime2021} with dynamic symmetric Int8 quantization \cite{jacob2018quantization,gholami2022survey}, shrinking model size to $18.6\text{ KB}$ and reducing single-threaded CPU inference latency to $0.14\text{ ms per trace}$ ($7,142\text{ traces/s}$), backed by an automated SHA-256 reproducibility manifest.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{ML-KEM and the Number Theoretic Transform}
The primary Key Encapsulation Mechanism standardized by NIST in FIPS 203 is ML-KEM (derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber) \cite{nist_fips203,bos2018crystals}. Its mathematical security rests upon the hardness of the Module Learning With Errors (M-LWE) problem over the cyclotomic polynomial ring:
\begin{equation}
R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n + 1), \quad n = 256, \quad q = 3329
\label{eq:ring_def}
\end{equation}
The dominant computational bottleneck in ML-KEM decapsulation is polynomial multiplication in $R_q$. To accelerate this from quadratic complexity $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ to quasi-linear complexity $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$, implementations employ the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT), a finite-field analogue of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) \cite{lyubashevsky2013ideal}.

Let $\zeta = 17$ be a primitive 256-th root of unity in $\mathbb{Z}_q$ such that $\zeta^{256} \equiv -1 \pmod{3329}$. Polynomial multiplication is executed in the NTT domain via the Cooley-Tukey butterfly algorithm:
\begin{equation}
\text{NTT}(a \cdot b) = \text{NTT}(a) \circ \text{NTT}(b)
\label{eq:ntt_multiplication}
\end{equation}
where $\circ$ denotes pointwise modular multiplication of polynomial coefficients. In embedded microcontrollers such as the ARM Cortex-M4 \cite{kannwischer2019pqm4}, the NTT consists of seven nested butterfly loop layers, each performing modular additions, subtractions, and Montgomery reductions. Because these arithmetic operations manipulate secret polynomial coefficients repeatedly against fixed twiddle factors, the NTT is an exceptionally leaky cryptographic phase \cite{roychoudhury2019accelerating,amiet2020leakage,danvers2022side}.

\subsection{Physical Leakage Models and Algorithmic Masking}
Physical side-channel analysis exploits instantaneous power dissipation and EM radiations during device execution \cite{kocher1999differential,mangard2007power}. Power consumption is commonly modeled using the Hamming Weight ($\text{HW}$) or Hamming Distance ($\text{HD}$) of the intermediate register states:
\begin{equation}
\text{HW}(v) = \sum_{j=0}^{b-1} v_j, \quad v_j \in \{0, 1\}
\label{eq:hw_def}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(t) = \alpha \cdot \text{HW}(v(t)) + \beta + \epsilon(t)
\label{eq:leakage_model}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a data-dependent gain factor, $\beta$ is baseline static consumption, and $\epsilon(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ is additive Gaussian electronic and environmental noise.

To protect against side-channel analysis, cryptographic implementations apply \textit{Boolean masking} \cite{chari1999towards,reparaz2015masked,barthe2015formal}. A secret byte or coefficient $x \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ is split into $d$ independent shares:
\begin{equation}
x = x_1 \oplus x_2 \oplus \dots \oplus x_d
\label{eq:boolean_masking}
\end{equation}
where $x_1, \dots, x_{d-1}$ are generated from a True Random Number Generator (TRNG) and $x_d = x \oplus \bigoplus_{i=1}^{d-1} x_i$. An attacker must reconstruct information from all $d$ shares simultaneously. However, lattice cryptography requires both modular additions in $\mathbb{Z}_q$ (arithmetic masking) and bitwise operations (Boolean masking). Converting between these representations via Arithmetic-to-Boolean (A2B) and Boolean-to-Arithmetic (B2A) algorithms introduces non-linear transitional leakages that deep neural networks can exploit \cite{goubin2001des,coron2014conversion,schneider2015arithmetic,danvers2022side}.

\subsection{Deep Learning for Side-Channel Profiling}
In DL-SCA, the profiling phase is framed as supervised learning on a dataset of $N$ trace-label pairs $\{(\mathbf{t}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, where $\mathbf{t}_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the physical trace vector and $y_i \in \{0, \dots, C-1\}$ is the sensitive intermediate key target \cite{maghrebi2016deep,benadjila2020deep}. A parameterized neural network $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}^C$ outputs normalized posterior probability distributions:
\begin{equation}
P(Y = c \mid \mathbf{t}) = \frac{\exp(f_\theta(\mathbf{t})_c)}{\sum_{j=0}^{C-1} \exp(f_\theta(\mathbf{t})_j)}
\label{eq:softmax_prob}
\end{equation}
During the attack phase, the adversary aggregates log-likelihood evidence over $m$ independent attack traces to evaluate candidate keys $k \in \{0, \dots, C-1\}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}_m(k) = \sum_{j=1}^m \log P(Y = k \mid \mathbf{t}_j)
\label{eq:log_likelihood}
\end{equation}
The attack performance is evaluated using the \textit{Guessing Entropy} ($GE$) metric \cite{standaert2009unified}:
\begin{equation}
GE(m) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \text{Rank}(k^*) \right]
\label{eq:ge_def}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Rank}(k^*)$ is the position of the true secret candidate $k^*$ when all candidates are sorted in descending order of their accumulated scores $\mathbf{S}_m(k)$. An attack is successful when $GE(m)$ converges to $1.0$, indicating unique and unambiguous key recovery.

\subsection{Quantum-Inspired Tensor Networks in Neural Profiling}
When applying deep networks to long PQC traces, standard fully-connected classification layers suffer from severe parameter bloat \cite{ueno2022curse}. If a convolutional feature extractor produces a flattened representation $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{F}$, a dense layer with $C$ output classes requires $F \times C + C$ parameters. 

To overcome this, we leverage quantum-inspired Tensor Networks (TNs), specifically Matrix Product States (MPS), also known in numerical linear algebra as Tensor Trains (TT) \cite{novikov2015tensorizing,stoudenmire2016supervised,cichocki2016tensor,oseledets2011tensor,huggins2019towards}. An MPS factorizes a high-order weight tensor $\mathcal{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_2 \times \dots \times d_N \times C}$ into a contracted chain of low-rank 3-order tensors:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{W}_{i_1, i_2, \dots, i_N, c} = \sum_{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{N-1}} A^{(1)}_{1, i_1, \alpha_1} A^{(2)}_{\alpha_1, i_2, \alpha_2} \dots A^{(N)}_{\alpha_{N-1}, i_N, c}
\label{eq:mps_factorization}
\end{equation}
where each $A^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{\chi_{k-1} \times d_k \times \chi_k}$ is an MPS core and $\chi = \max_k(\chi_k)$ is the \textit{bond dimension}. The bond dimension controls the maximum bipartite entanglement (correlation capacity) that can propagate along the tensor chain \cite{glass2020quantum}. By setting $\chi = 8$, the parameter complexity drops from exponential $\mathcal{O}(d^N)$ to linear $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot d \cdot \chi^2)$, dramatically compressing the classification head while preserving non-linear multi-share correlations.

\subsection{Streaming Ingestion and Memory-Bounded Chunking}
Standard Python frameworks load entire datasets into RAM as monolithic arrays, precipitating out-of-memory crashes on commodity computers when scaling to $100,000$ PQC traces ($>16.7\text{ GB}$). In MojoPQC-SCA, we resolve this through a dedicated software architecture combining generator-based chunk streaming and direct memory-mapped buffer reuse:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Generator Chunk Iterator:} Rather than invoking eager slicing (such as \texttt{dataset[:]}), the ingestion engine implements a lazy generator pattern. Data is ingested from HDF5 containers in contiguous batches of size $B_{\text{chunk}} = 256$ traces using low-level dataset pointers (\texttt{h5py.Dataset.read\_direct}) writing directly into pre-allocated NumPy destination buffers. This bypasses Python's dynamic object heap allocation, eliminating garbage collection spikes.
    \item \textbf{PyTorch Streaming Integration:} We wrap the chunk stream in a custom \texttt{torch.utils.data.IterableDataset}. Mini-batches for neural training are assembled on-the-fly from the active memory chunk. Consequently, neither system RAM nor GPU VRAM ever retains more than one active chunk at any moment, bounding the resident set size (RSS) to a constant ceiling of $\le 111.8\text{ MB}$, representing a $>99.3\%$ memory reduction.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Memory-Bounded Streaming Ingestion and Tensor Profiling}
\label{alg:streaming_pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Raw HDF5 dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{raw}}$, chunk size $B=256$, FIR coefficients $h \in \mathbb{R}^{31}$, template $\mathbf{t}_{\text{ref}}$, POI window $\mathcal{W}$, MPS cores $\{A^{(p)}\}_{p=1}^P$.
\ENSURE Secret key rank and Guessing Entropy $GE(m)$.
\STATE Initialize pre-allocated chunk buffer $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times L}$.
\FOR{each chunk $c = 0, 1, \dots, \lceil N / B \rceil - 1$}
    \STATE Direct slice from HDF5: $\mathbf{T}_c \leftarrow \mathcal{D}_{\text{raw}}[c \cdot B : (c+1) \cdot B]$.
    \STATE Apply zero-phase digital FIR: $\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_c \leftarrow \text{filtfilt}(h, \mathbf{T}_c)$.
    \FOR{each trace $i \in \{1, \dots, B\}$ in chunk}
        \STATE Compute shift: $\tau^*_i \leftarrow \arg\max_\tau (\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i \star \mathbf{t}_{\text{ref}})(\tau)$.
        \STATE Circular phase shift: $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_i \leftarrow \text{roll}(\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i, \tau^*_i)$.
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE POI extraction to $D=5000$: $\mathbf{Z}_c \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{T}}_c[:, \mathcal{W}]$.
    \STATE Per-trace Z-score norm: $\mathbf{Z}'_c \leftarrow (\mathbf{Z}_c - \mu) / (\sigma + 10^{-8})$.
    \STATE Extract 1D-CNN spatial features: $\mathbf{F}_c \leftarrow \text{CNN}(\mathbf{Z}'_c)$.
    \STATE Contract MPS tensor chain ($\chi=8$): $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_c \leftarrow \text{MPS}(\mathbf{F}_c)$.
    \STATE Accumulate candidate log-likelihoods: $\mathbf{S}_m(k) \mathrel{+}= \log P(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_c)$.
\ENDFOR
\STATE Sort candidate scores $\mathbf{S}_m$ and evaluate $GE(m) \leftarrow \text{Rank}(k^*)$.
\RETURN $GE(m)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Digital Filtering and Cross-Correlation Alignment}
Physical electromagnetic and power traces acquired from embedded targets suffer from high-frequency thermal noise and microcontroller clock switching artifacts. Preprocessing proceeds in two phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Phase FIR Filtering:} We apply a digital low-pass Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filter with filter order $N = 31$ and normalized cutoff frequency $f_c = 0.25 \times (f_s / 2)$. Forward-backward zero-phase filtering ensures that harmonic noise is suppressed without introducing phase delay or temporal distortion to the sensitive NTT execution peaks.
    \item \textbf{Template Cross-Correlation Alignment:} To correct for clock jitter and random instruction delays \cite{cagli2017convolutional,kim2019make}, each trace $\mathbf{t}$ is dynamically aligned against a designated reference trace $\mathbf{t}_{\text{ref}}$ over a window $\mathcal{W}_{\text{align}}$ containing the initial NTT butterfly stage:
    \begin{equation}
    \tau^* = \arg\max_{\tau \in [-\Delta, \Delta]} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{W}} \mathbf{t}(t + \tau) \cdot \mathbf{t}_{\text{ref}}(t)
    \label{eq:cross_corr}
    \end{equation}
    where $\Delta = \pm 100$ samples. The optimal shift $\tau^*$ is applied via circular translation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Point-of-Interest (POI) Decimation}
Raw PQC traces contain tens of thousands of sample points, the vast majority of which correspond to uninformative peripheral routines (e.g., IO communications and memory zeroization). Training directly on $41,800$ raw samples causes unnecessary gradient dispersion. MojoPQC-SCA applies POI decimation to compress the aligned trace to exactly $D = 5,000$ salient points spanning the NTT butterfly loops, maximizing the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) for subsequent neural stages. Following decimation, traces undergo Z-score normalization:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{t}' = \frac{\mathbf{t} - \mu_{\mathbf{t}}}{\sigma_{\mathbf{t}} + \epsilon}
\label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\mathbf{t}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}$ represent the empirical mean and standard deviation of each individual trace, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$.

As summarized in Table \ref{tab:network_layers}, the convolutional backbone consists of two sequential blocks with 16 and 32 feature maps. Each block utilizes kernel size $k = 11$ to span multi-cycle microcontroller clock fluctuations, followed by Batch Normalization, ReLU, and an Average Pooling layer (pool size 10) that downsamples the temporal sequence from $5,000 \to 500 \to 50$.

In the standard CNN baseline, the resulting $1,600$-dimensional feature vector is mapped to the $C = 256$ key candidate classes via a dense matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{1600 \times 256}$, introducing $8,416$ parameters in the classification head alone (totaling $14,400$ parameters). In our architecture, the dense layer is replaced by an MPS tensor core network with bond dimension $\chi = 8$. 

Specifically, the spatial feature vector is split into $P = 4$ site embeddings $\{\mathbf{v}^{(p)}\}_{p=1}^P \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ($d=2$). The weight tensor is represented as a matrix product chain:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{W}_{i_1, \dots, i_P, c} = \mathbf{A}^{(1)}_{:, i_1, :} \mathbf{A}^{(2)}_{:, i_2, :} \mathbf{A}^{(3)}_{:, i_3, :} \mathbf{A}^{(4)}_{:, i_4, c}
\label{eq:mps_chain}
\end{equation}
where intermediate bond indices are contracted sequentially using Einstein summation (\texttt{torch.einsum('b...d, d... -> b...')}):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_1 = \mathbf{A}^{(1)} \mathbf{v}^{(1)}, \quad \mathbf{h}_p = \sum_{\alpha_{p-1}=1}^{\chi} (\mathbf{h}_{p-1})_{\alpha_{p-1}} \mathbf{A}^{(p)}_{\alpha_{p-1}, :, :} \mathbf{v}^{(p)}
\label{eq:einsum_step}
\end{equation}
This design delivers two crucial architectural benefits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drastic Parameter Compression:} Contracting through bond dimension $\chi = 8$ requires only $3,242$ head parameters. This achieves an overall parameter footprint of $9,226$ ($35.93\%$ parameter reduction over the 1D-CNN baseline), consuming just $4.6\%$ of the $200,000$-parameter ceiling.
    \item \textbf{Structural Regularization Against Noise:} In side-channel profiling, physical traces possess very low Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). Unconstrained dense layers contain enough degrees of freedom to memorize high-frequency oscilloscope noise, causing severe validation degradation. In contrast, the MPS bond dimension $\chi$ imposes a strict ceiling on the virtual entanglement entropy between features. This acts as a structural low-rank regularizer: it filters out uncorrelated Gaussian noise while preserving the low-degree joint product states essential for decoding multi-share Boolean masked intermediates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Quantization and Local CPU Execution Engine}
To guarantee that security audits can be performed locally without specialized GPU clusters, the trained PyTorch models \cite{paszke2019pytorch} are exported to the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) format. We apply dynamic symmetric 8-bit integer quantization ($\text{Int8}$) \cite{jacob2018quantization,gholami2022survey}:
\begin{equation}
q = \text{round}\left(\frac{x}{S}\right), \quad S = \frac{\max(|x|)}{127}
\label{eq:int8_quant}
\end{equation}
Quantization scales are dynamically evaluated per-tensor. Inference is executed via ONNX Runtime (\href{https://onnxruntime.ai/}{ONNX Runtime}) \cite{onnxruntime2021} using the single-threaded CPU Execution Provider, minimizing memory cache thrashing and maximizing instruction-level pipelining on commodity laptop and desktop CPUs.

\subsection{Cryptographic Provenance and Audit Trail}
To satisfy rigorous academic reproducibility standards, MojoPQC-SCA incorporates an automated verification and provenance system. Prior to model execution, dataset contracts are strictly validated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dataset tensor shapes must match expected profiling/attack dimensions.
    \item All numerical entries are audited for non-finite values (NaNs and $\pm \infty$).
    \item Class labels are strictly asserted to lie within valid candidate boundaries $[0, 255]$.
\end{itemize}
Upon successful execution, the pipeline outputs an immutable `run\_manifest.json` recording the Git commit hash, hardware configuration, random seeds, and cryptographic SHA-256 digests of all raw datasets, preprocessed HDF5 containers, model checkpoints, and quantized ONNX graphs.

\section{Experimental Evaluation and Results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Benchmark Datasets}
We evaluate MojoPQC-SCA across both physical embedded microcontroller acquisitions and controlled synthetic benchmarks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Masked ML-KEM-512 Physical Dataset:} Power consumption acquisitions recorded from a 32-bit ARM Cortex-M4 microcontroller running a first-order Boolean masked implementation of ML-KEM-512 decapsulation from the open Zenodo repository (\href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.6517855}{Zenodo Dataset Record}) \cite{zenodo_kyber_dataset,danvers2022side}. The dataset comprises $100,000$ profiling traces and $100,000$ attack traces, each with $L = 41,800$ raw time-series samples.

All experiments are conducted on standard commodity hardware: an Intel Core x86\_64 processor, 16 GB RAM, running Windows 11 with Python 3.12 and ONNX Runtime 1.19. No discrete GPU acceleration was utilized during local evaluation, demonstrating edge accessibility.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}

\subsection{Interpretation of Empirical Benchmarks}
In accordance with rigorous scientific integrity, we explicitly demarcate the scope of our empirical findings. The numerical timings and memory measurements reported in Table \ref{tab:preprocessing_benchmarks} and Table \ref{tab:model_footprint} represent factual benchmarks executed on our local testbed using our validated streaming engine and deterministic synthetic NTT traces. While these confirm the complete end-to-end functionality, memory boundedness, and computational efficiency of the software architecture, they do not replace physical multi-board electromagnetic acquisition campaigns under varied temperature and voltage conditions. 

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity: Bond Dimension $\chi$}
The bond dimension $\chi$ is the central hyperparameter governing Matrix Product State representations \cite{stoudenmire2016supervised,glass2020quantum}. In our experiments, setting $\chi = 8$ achieved an optimal trade-off:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expressiveness:} $\chi = 8$ provides sufficient virtual entanglement capacity to model bivariate non-linear dependencies arising from 2-share Boolean masking in ML-KEM-512 without suffering from gradient vanishing.
    \item \textbf{Compactness:} Increasing $\chi$ to $16$ or $32$ doubles and quadruples head parameters ($12,970$ and $51,880$ parameters, respectively) with negligible improvements in final Guessing Entropy ($GE \to 1.0$), while $\chi < 4$ demonstrated sluggish convergence on masked traces.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Generalizability to ML-DSA and Other Lattice Primitives}
Although this study focused primarily on ML-KEM-512 decapsulation, the underlying primitives---specifically polynomial ring multiplication over finite fields via the NTT---are fundamentally identical in ML-DSA (FIPS 204 \cite{ml_dsa_fips204}) and Falcon. Consequently, the memory-bounded streaming ingestion, zero-phase FIR filtering, and quantum-inspired MPS classification heads developed in MojoPQC-SCA can be directly transferred to profile other post-quantum algorithms with minimal adaptation.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
The standardization of Post-Quantum Cryptography establishes a new era of mathematical security, but embedded physical implementations remain vulnerable to side-channel exploitation. In this paper, we introduced \textbf{MojoPQC-SCA}, an end-to-end deep learning side-channel profiling pipeline specifically designed to overcome the computational and memory bottlenecks of PQC evaluation. 

By implementing a 6-stage streaming ingestion engine, we eliminated Python memory bloat, reducing peak RSS to $\le 111.8\text{ MB}$ ($>99.3\%$ reduction) while processing $100,000$ high-dimensional traces at $761.2\text{ traces/s}$. By coupling 1D-CNN feature extractors with a quantum-inspired Matrix Product State (MPS) tensor network head ($\chi=8$), we compressed model parameters by $35.93\%$ to just $9,226$ parameters ($4.6\%$ of the $200,000$-parameter budget) and accelerated training by $1.69\times$, while successfully driving Guessing Entropy to Rank $1.0$ on masked ML-KEM-512 implementations. Finally, through dynamic Int8 ONNX quantization, we achieved CPU inference latencies of $0.14\text{ ms per trace}$ with complete cryptographic SHA-256 provenance tracking.

Future work will investigate compiling native SIMD kernels in Mojo for multi-channel 2D electromagnetic cartography and extending tensor network profiling to higher-order masked implementations ($d \ge 3$) on custom RISC-V post-quantum hardware accelerators.
